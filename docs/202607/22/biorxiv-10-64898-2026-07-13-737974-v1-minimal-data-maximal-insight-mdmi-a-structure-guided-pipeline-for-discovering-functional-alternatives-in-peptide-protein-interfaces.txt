Abstract 20 

Discovering functional peptides across vast sequence space remains a formidable challenge, 21 

particularly when experimental training data is scarce. We present Minimal Data Maximal Insight 22 

(MDMI), a two-stage structure-guided computational pipeline that designs functional peptide variants 23 

using only a small, annotated dataset. Rather than relying on sequence information alone, MDMI 24 

integrates three-dimensional structural features derived from predicted peptide-protein complexes into 25 

a machine learning model that captures interface geometry and binding energetics. This structure-26 

aware predictor, paired with a genetic algorithm for sequence exploration, reduced false positives from 27 

70% to close to zero in an all-negative benchmark panel compared with a sequence-only model in 28 

computational benchmarking, and produced approximately four-fold more high-confidence in silico 29 

binders than state-of-the-art peptide/protein design baselines. Using the split-GFP system as a testbed, 30 

where fluorescence provides a direct functional readout of peptide -protein complementation, MDMI 31 

identified peptides with up to 38% sequence divergence from wild-type in Stage 1 while retaining 32 

measurable activity. In Stage 2, motif-guided recombination of successful Stage 1 variants produced 33 

highly divergent yet functional peptides bearing over 50% sequence difference from wild-type, revealing 34 

two distinct functional clusters in sequence space. As further validation, a top-performing candidate 35 

expressed as a full-length GFP fusion retained a GFP-like emission profile, supporting formation of a 36 

fluorescent GFP-like scaffold. These results demonstrate that structure-informed pipelines can uncover 37 

remote functional sequence space from minimal data, with broad implications for peptide and 38 

therapeutic analog discovery. 39

## 3

Introduction 41 

Peptides, short chains of typically 2 to 50 amino acids, participate in a wide range of biological 42 

processes, from endocrine signaling and neurotransmission to innate immunity and host defense [1] .43 

Such peptides mediate a significant fraction of protein-protein interactions in cells (estimated up to 44 

~40%) [2] and have gained prominence as therapeutics and research tools. Clinical interest in these 45 

molecules has surged, driven by their favorable pharmacology, enhanced tissue penetration, low 46 

immunogenicity as well as simpler solid-phase synthesis and the commercial success of peptide drugs 47 

such as GLP-1 receptor agonists [3–8] .48 

Despite this promise, systematically exploring the vast sequence space of peptides for new binders 49 

remains a formidable challenge. A peptide of moderate length (e.g., 20 amino acids) has on the order 50 

of 20 20 possible permutations if only considering natural amino acids, an astronomical diversity that far 51 

outstrips what can be tested experimentally. Brute-force library screening methods (such as phage 52 

display or yeast display) can sample up to ~10 9-10 11 variants in a single experiment, which is an 53 

infinitesimal fraction of all sequences [9,10] . In practice, peptide and protein libraries are often designed 54 

as focused or semi-rational libraries around known sequences or consensus motifs, since fully random 55 

sequence libraries contain only a very small fraction of folded or functional variants [11,12] .56 

Machine-learning (ML) frameworks such as RFpeptide [13] , Pepflow [14] , PepMLM [15] , and Peptune [16] have 57 

been developed to narrow the search and accelerate design. Most are optimized primarily around 58 

binding, activity labels, or sequence plausibility, rather than around the precise bound geometry 59 

required for downstream functionality. Yet several practical constraints often limit ML-guided peptide 60 

design. First, most architectures demand thousands of labeled examples to generalize [17,18] . Attaining 61 

such large-scale experimentally measured properties (e.g., binding affinity, antimicrobial potency, or 62 

cell permeability) is rarely attainable for new peptide targets, especially outside of industry [19] . When 63 

only a few dozen such pairs exist, models tend to memorize rather than extrapolate [20] .64 

Moreover, unlike large proteins, peptides lack extensive structural databases to inform modeling. The 65 

Protein Data Bank contains hundreds of thousands of protein structures, but relatively few high-66 

resolution peptide-protein complex structures [21,22] . This scarcity of structural data is problematic 67 

because short peptides typically do not adopt a fixed folded structure on their own; their functional 68 

conformations emerge upon binding to partner proteins [23–25] . Ignoring the context of the target interface 69 

can therefore mislead optimization, especially for flexible peptides that can bind in multiple 70 

conformations (so-called degenerate binding modes). Indeed, peptide activity is often highly context-71 

dependent, and purely sequence-based design may stumble if a peptide’s binding modes or interface 72 

contacts are not explicitly considered [26–28] .73 

Finally, purely data-driven or deep generative models (e.g. large protein language models or diffusion-74 

based protein designers) may struggle to propose truly diverse solutions: they tend to generate 75 

sequences similar or close to those seen in training or in natural proteins, and without careful tuning 76 

they might miss highly divergent yet functional peptides [29–31] . Overall, the dual problems of enormous 77

data is limited, with application in the discovery of novel peptide and therapeutic analogs that remain 127 

functional even at a distance from known sequences. 128 

## 129 

Results 130 

We developed a two-stage strategy, Minimal Data · Maximal Insight (MDMI), to identify functional but 131 

highly divergent GFP 11 variants. In Stage 1, a structure-aware machine-learning model trained on a 132 

minimal public dataset guided a genetic algorithm toward functional sequence space, generating 133 

candidates with 3 to 6 mutations predicted to retain fluorescence. The top candidates were synthesized 134 

and screened experimentally. In Stage 2, recurring beneficial mutations from the validated Stage 1 hits 135 

were recombined to construct new peptides. This yielded variants with >50% sequence divergence 136 

from wild-type GFP 11 that still complemented GFP 1-10 with strong fluorescence, revealing multiple 137 

distinct sequence solutions for preserving function. 138 

## 139 

Stage 1: Structure-Aware Modeling Enables Enrichment of Functional Variants 140 

We hypothesized that explicit integration of structural interaction data could overcome the data scarcity 141 

limitations for peptide binder design. To test this, we first drew on a publicly available Aequorea victoria 142 

GFP ( av GFP) mutant dataset providing brightness measurements [34] . From this resource, we filtered 143 

109 variants bearing substitutions exclusively in the GFP 11 segment (aa 215 - aa 230). Our workflow 144 

comprised two main components: a predictive model and a generative model (Fig. 1b-c). 145

We first trained a regressor on continuous fluorescence intensities (labels) derived solely from the 160

peptides only at evaluation time. This two-step paradigm is widely used in quantitative-structure-activity-162 

relationship (QSAR) modeling, where models are trained on continuous activity values and a biologically 163 

defined activity threshold is applied afterward to classify compounds as active or inactive [41,42] . In 164 

practice, the regressor produces a continuous score, and the activity cutoff acts as a post-hoc 165 

classification threshold. To validate that structural features provide essential discrimination capability 166 

beyond sequence information alone, we tested model specificity using non-binding peptides against a 167 

sequence-based model. This test reveals the model's tendency to incorrectly classify non-binders as 168 

positive hits, a critical concern in drug discovery where false positives waste experimental resources. 169 

The sequence-only model exhibited a 70% false positive rate (7/10 negatives misclassified), whereas 170 

the structure-aware model (SPServer only) achieved 20% false positive rate (2/10 negatives 171 

misclassified), demonstrating that structural features capture critical binding determinants that 172 

sequence patterns cannot reliably identify. Adding an ensemble layer that combined an XGBoost 173 

regressor trained on the original structural features with a KNN regressor operating on VAE-derived 174

Genetic Algorithm for Sequence Generation 194 

With the goal of creating peptides that have limited similarity to the original (wild-type) peptide, in parallel 195 

we implemented a genetic algorithm via the PyGAD framework [43] . The genetic algorithm served to 196 

explore the broader sequence space systematically, drawing on our predictive model to guide which 197 

productive mutations to retain (Fig. 2). Beginning with a randomized population of peptide sequences, 198 

each peptide was evaluated for its predicted brightness and complex stability with GFP 1-10 . Sequences 199 

with greater fitness underwent crossover and mutation operations, thereby creating offspring peptide 200 

sequences that inherit advantageous structural and functional traits. By iterating through several 201 

generations, the genetic algorithm converged on top -ranked variants, which were then prioritized for 202 

empirical validation. During selection, predicted solubility was assessed using the CamSol [44] web 203 

server, and only candidates with both favorable solubility scores and high predicted brightness were 204 

retained for wet-lab testing. 205

Retraining Our Model Using In-House Data 211 

After we established confidence in the predictive model using a public dataset (Fig. 1d) [34] , we then used 212 

the model to generate new candidate sequences, which were subsequently evaluated by in-house 213 

functional screening across a range of fluorescence outputs (Supplementary Fig. S1). Building on this, 214 

we generated our own experimental dataset to retrain the model and implemented a genetic algorithm 215 

to design new sequences. The original dataset, sourced from the literature [34] , involved in vivo 216 

expression in E. coli of av GFP mutants. We hypothesized that a dataset collected under conditions 217 

consistent with our in vitro experiments would enhance the model's performance. 218 

Consequently, we screened 114 GFP 11 variants comprising sequences generated by the initial model 219 

trained on the public dataset, together with randomly mutated control sequences. Their fluorescence 220 

was measured relative to wild-type GFP 11 after complementation with purified superfolder GFP 1-10 in a 221 

cell-free expression assay, generating the in-house dataset used for retraining. 222 

The screening results and position-wise amino-acid distributions for the 114-sequence in-house 223 

retraining set are shown in Fig. 3a-b. Figure 3c reports the positional distribution of substitutions across 224 

the entire screened library. Substitutions were concentrated at positions 7, 9, and 11 (110/114, 113/114, 225 

and 109/114 variants, respectively), while the other mutable positions were represented at lower but 226 

broadly similar frequencies (16 to 59 variants per site). The glutamate residue (E8) in the center of the 227 

peptide plays an important role in coordinating the chromophore responsible for fluorescence and was 228 

held invariant by design [45,46] . Given the role of the E8 region in chromophore formation, variation at 229 

adjacent residues is expected to affect fluorescence in both positive and negative ways. Although the 230 

wild-type GFP 11 is moderately polar (GRAVY score of -0.10) [47] , our mutant peptides span the entire 231 

spectrum: some retain a balanced profile (51/114), while others skewed slightly toward hydrophobic 232 

(47/114) or hydrophilic (16/114) content (Fig. 3d). 233 

Most peptide variants were substantially dimmer than wild-type GFP 11 , although a small subset matched 234 

or exceeded wild-type brightness. As a result, the retraining set contained examples spanning strongly 235 

deleterious to strongly enhancing sequence changes, allowing the model to learn from graded 236 

fluorescence outcomes rather than binary labels. At the same time, the strong skew toward weak 237 

variants meant that the retraining set was dominated by low-activity examples, leaving relatively sparse 238 

coverage of the highest-performing sequences. 239

Stage 2: Motif-Guided Assembly of Highly Divergent Variants 383 

Encouraged by Stage 1’s ability to find functional peptides with up to 38% divergence (3-6 mutations; 384 

Fig. 5-6), we next decided to test a higher degree (>50%) of sequence divergence from wild-type GFP 11 .385 

Such highly mutated sequences are attractive for discovering alternative binding modes, orthogonal 386 

peptide tools, or flexibility around intellectual property but they represent a remote sequence space 387 

where machine learning predictions are often unreliable. This challenge was particularly stringent 388 

because the predictor had been trained predominantly on peptide variants with 5 to 7 mutations from 389 

wild-type GFP 11 (median = 6 mutations, 37.5% divergence; IQR = 6 to 7 mutations), with only sparse 390 

coverage of more highly divergent variants (8 to 9 mutations) and these highly divergent variants were 391 

uniformly weak in the assay. Consistent with this limitation, when we directly asked the model to 392 

propose GFP 11 sequences with >50% mutations (i.e. 8 or more substitutions), the resulting designs 393 

uniformly failed to reconstitute fluorescence (Supplementary Fig. S3). 394 

These results highlight a practical limit of purely algorithmic design: once candidate sequences move 395 

well beyond the mutational regime represented in the training data, prediction becomes an extrapolation 396 

problem and both false positives and false negatives become more likely. Similar generalization 397 

challenges have been seen in other ML-guided protein design studies, where models can extrapolate 398 

modestly beyond their training regime but show a marked drop in functional yield when pushed deeper 399 

into sequence space [51] . To overcome this hurdle, we returned to leverage motif-guided recombination 400 

of the best Stage 1 variants. We hypothesized that, while our single model-driven attempt to jump to 401 

>50% sequence divergence was not successful, the clues about how to build a functional but distantly 402 

related peptide may be contained within the designs of successful moderate GFP 11 mutants. By 403 

analyzing the top-performing variants, defined here as those retaining >60% of wild-type fluorescence, 404

## 447 

Discussion 448 

Data scarcity has long hampered predictive modeling of proteins and peptides because the 449 

combinatorial explosion of admissible sequences quickly outpaces feasible wet-lab throughput. Prior 450 

approaches either relied on pretrained language models that assume the target sequence distribution 451 

resembles natural proteins, or on large, supervised datasets that are generally unattainable for newly 452 

emergent targets. In contrast, MDMI exploits a structure-aware model from a few dozen labeled 453 

examples. The success we observe with the split-GFP system suggests that high-quality, interaction-454 

aware features can compensate for limited sample size; this principle will likely translate to other 455 

peptide-protein interfaces provided that the complex can be modeled with reasonable confidence. 456 

In benchmarking against state-of-the-art models (e.g. RFdiffusion and PepMLM), MDMI produced 457 

approximately four-fold more high-quality binder sequences that exceeded an iPTM threshold of 0.7. 458 

This improvement is notable because all three methods were evaluated using the same AlphaFold-459 

Multimer confidence metrics. The higher hit rate therefore reflects the fact that MDMI proposes a 460 

candidate pool that is already enriched for sequences compatible with a high-confidence GFP 1-10 461 

interface, whereas RFdiffusion and PepMLM generate broader sets of candidates that contain many 462 

more low-confidence designs. 463 

Our results demonstrate that MDMI can navigate peptide sequence space effectively when only ~100 464 

experimentally annotated variants are available. By employing features derived from protein-peptide 465 

complex scoring, the workflow retained sufficient sensitivity to rank-order GFP 11 mutants 466 

(AUROC = 0.91, Fig. 1d) and to steer a genetic algorithm that yielded functional variants (Fig. 5). In the 467 

second stage of the workflow, motif-guided recombination of successful Stage 1 designs produced 468 

functional peptides with >50% sequence divergence from wild-type GFP 11 (Fig. 7). This two-stage 469 

strategy therefore expanded the search beyond the local mutational neighborhood while avoiding 470 

overreliance on direct extrapolation. The highly mutated sequences were manually assembled by 471 

combining residues that were repeatedly enriched among the top hits, which currently limits throughput 472

Although MDMI succeeds in a controlled split-GFP setting, several caveats merit attention. First, the 478 

workflow inherits the uncertainty of single-structure prediction: AlphaFold-Multimer provides a single 479 

dominant pose for each peptide-target pair, but peptide binding in solution can involve conformational 480 

heterogeneity, side-chain repacking, and local induced fit. Consequently, some candidates may appear 481 

favorable because of one high-scoring static pose, even though that interaction is not stable across an 482 

ensemble of conformations. This issue is especially important for highly mutated peptides, where 483 

binding may depend on subtle induced-fit effects. Future versions of MDMI could address this by 484 

incorporating features derived from docking and molecular dynamics. 485 

Second, the computational cost of structure prediction remains non-trivial ( ∼5 min per sequence on a 486 

high-end GPU, e.g., NVIDIA® RTX™ A6000). A more scalable next step would be to use multi-stage 487 

screening, in which inexpensive surrogate models or sequence-level filters triage large candidate pools 488 

and AlphaFold-Multimer is reserved for a smaller, higher-value subset. 489 

Third, looking toward the future with the incorporation of non-natural amino acids for therapeutic peptide 490 

design, our experiments focus on canonical amino acids; extending MDMI to non-natural residues will 491 

likely require explicit parameterization of those residues in the underlying modeling stack, including new 492 

residue templates, rotamer libraries, and score-function or charge terms, or alternatively the use of 493 

learned physicochemical-property surrogates derived from chemical structure [52–54] . Enabling non-494 

natural residues would substantially broaden the accessible design space and could allow MDMI to 495 

optimize not only binding, but also properties such as proteolytic stability, bioavailability, and target 496 

selectivity. Finally, although the genetic algorithm achieved substantial enrichment, alternative search 497 

strategies such as Bayesian optimization and reinforcement learning have also been applied to protein 498 

and peptide sequence design and may further accelerate convergence in larger sequence spaces [55–

Methods 516 

Predictive Model 517 

The predictive framework comprises a preprocessing pipeline and a two-branch neural network that 518 

integrates structural, statistical, and sequence-based features to predict fluorescence output for novel 519 

GFP 11 variants. 520 

## 521 

Data Preprocessing 522 

The dataset was partitioned pseudo-randomly into training (80%), validation (10%), and test (10%) sets 523 

using scikit-learn’s train_test_split utility. To maximize training data for model learning, the validation 524 

and test subsets were drawn from the original 20% holdout. Structural features were generated via 525 

Local ColabFold, a ColabFold implementation of AlphaFold2 utilizing MMseqs2 for multiple sequence 526 

alignment. GFP 11 sequences were concatenated to GFP 1-10 with a chain break indicator and processed 527 

in batches. Resulting PDB structures were scored using SPServer to extract 12 global normalized 528 

statistical potentials that quantify interfacial quality and penetration metrics. Additional energy terms 529 

and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) metrics were computed using PyRosetta’s scoring 530 

functions. In total, 49 structural and statistical features were compiled. 531 

To reduce model complexity, we performed feature importance analysis using ensemble approaches 532 

including SMOTE with random forest, gradient boosting, and XGBoost with custom loss weighting. The 533 

top 14 features, six from SPServer (Es3dc, E3dc, E3d, ZEcomb, ZEloca, ZE3dc) and eight from 534 

PyRosetta (fa_intra_sol_xover4, fa_rep, hbond_lr_bb, pro_close, average_energy, std_energy, 535 

total_energy, separated_sasa), were selected. All features were standardized to zero mean and unit 536 

variance using scikit-learn’s StandardScaler fitted on the training set and applied to validation and test 537 

sets. 538 

## 539 

Variational Autoencoder (VAE) 540 

A variational autoencoder was implemented in PyTorch Lightning to learn a compressed representation 541 

of the features. The encoder and decoder comprised alternating linear layers, one-dimensional batch 542 

normalization, and SELU activations, with a latent bottleneck of 16 dimensions. The encoder outputs 543 

parameterized mean (μ) and log-variance (log σ²) vectors, from which latent samples were drawn via 544 

the reparameterization trick. The VAE loss function combined mean squared reconstruction error, 545 

Kullback-Leibler divergence, and two regularization terms: a distance loss encouraging alignment 546 

between normalized latent distances and label-derived distances, and a line loss preventing linear 547 

correlation between latents and inputs. The relative weight of the distance term was increased 548 

progressively during training to promote informative latent representations. Optimization employed 549 

Adam (learning rate=0.01, weight decay=1e-5) with a learning rate scheduler (γ=0.993). Early stopping 550 

was applied with patience of 100 epochs and a minimum δ of 0.01; training was limited to 1,000 epochs. 551 

The best-performing model on the validation set was retained. 552

## 20 

Fully Connected Neural Network (FCNN )554 

Encoded latent vectors (16 dimensions) were input to a fully connected neural network for regression. 555 

The network, also in PyTorch Lightning, comprised linear layers of sizes 32, 64, 128, and 256, 556 

interleaved with batch normalization, SELU activations, and a dropout layer (p=0.2) after the first hidden 557 

layer. A final sigmoid activation scaled outputs to match the fluorescence range. Training utilized Adam 558 

(learning rate=0.002, weight decay=1e-6) with a scheduler (γ=0.98) and early stopping (patience=30, 559 

δ=0.01), for up to 100 epochs. 560 

## 561 

pLM-Based Branch 562 

A pre-trained protein language model was fine-tuned with a custom fine-tuning step as a way to provide 563 

information about evolutionary fitness. Based on work by Biswas et al. [50] , ProtBERT was trained using 564 

“evo-tuning”, using sequences from the fluorescent protein family to learn the distinct features of our 565 

target protein GFP 11 . Afterwards, a regular fine-tuning approach was applied to train a top-level model 566 

with experimentally determined data about GFP 11 functionality. After training, generated protein 567 

embeddings from the last hidden layer outputs with a CLS pooling scheme were used to help guide the 568 

full predictor model. To make sure the weight of these features did not overwhelm the results from the 569 

structurally informed models, we trained a small auto-encoder model to compress the embeddings 570 

down to the twelve most important features, which were combined with the structural features in the 571 

VAE. 572 

## 573 

Genetic Algorithm 574 

To explore sequence space and optimize GFP 11 fluorescence, we implemented a genetic algorithm 575 

using PyGAD [43] . An initial population of 20 randomly generated 16-mer sequences was encoded via 576 

the VAE and evaluated by the FCNN. After each generation, mutation statistics were updated in a 577 

dictionary mapping each residue substitution to a fitness weight. New sequences were generated by 578 

mutating the top 10 performers, using weighted probabilities that balanced exploratory random 579 

mutations and exploitation of successful substitutions. Mutation probabilities transitioned from uniform 580 

to dictionary-informed as the weight factor increased over generations. In the final optimization phase, 581 

mutation donors were selected from a tree structure of all prior generations. Each generation node 582 

scored by (mean top-ten fitness), success rate, and branching factor determined a propensity score. 583 

This approach preserved promising lineages while avoiding stagnation in low-fitness branches. 584 

## 585 

Implementation and Computational Resources 586 

All genetic algorithm computations and network inferences were executed on a workstation (32 cores, 587 

256 GB RAM, NVIDIA RTX A6000). Sequence preprocessing and model evaluation required ~5 588 

minutes per sequence. A parallel work-pool architecture on Google Cloud VMs, coordinated via 589 

MongoDB, enabled near-linear scaling across 20 worker nodes. 590
