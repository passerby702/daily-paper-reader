# Pathogen context reshapes antimicrobial peptide generation 

Shiyang You 1, Chen Zhang 2, Yingchun Han 3, Qiuyun Jiang 3, Xuan Guo 1,Mingjia Li 1, Yongxin Su 1, Xiyang Dong 3,* , Menglin Yang 1,* & Hongyuan Lu 1,*

Abstract. Antimicrobial peptides are a promising source of new anti-infective agents, but 1

their discovery is limited by a practical bottleneck: selecting a small set of candidates 2

to synthesize and test for a specific pathogen. Existing peptide generators can expand 3

antimicrobial-like sequence space, but most leave pathogen specificity to predictors or filters 4

after generation. Here, we use AMPHORA, a pathogen-conditioned sequence–structure 5

generator, to build antimicrobial peptide libraries shaped by target context. AMPHORA 6

couples a short-peptide representation model to latent-flow generation conditioned on 7

target class, genome-derived features and strain-description text. In matched, partial 8

and shuffled controls, pathogen context redirected generated peptide pools beyond broad 9

activity labels, with the strongest effects when genome and text conditions were combined. 10 

Counterfactual generation from the same initial noise showed that strain descriptions 11 

primarily changed amino-acid choices, whereas genome-derived features contributed more 12 

strongly to predicted structural properties. Across species, AMPHORA produced target-13 

dependent enrichment, and matched bacterial context shifted APEX-predicted potency-14 

score distributions compared with class-only generation. The generated peptides remained 15 

diverse, novel and peptide-like by sequence and predicted-structure analyses. Collectively, 16 

our results establish pathogen context as an active design signal for antimicrobial peptide 17 

generation and provide a target-aware route to pre-synthesis library design. 18

Introduction 19 

Antimicrobial peptides are a promising source of new anti-infective molecules [1] . Machine-20 

learning screens have expanded the accessible AMP search space across large biological 21 

sequence collections [2] . A major bottleneck arises before activity is measured: a laboratory 22 

must choose a small set of candidates to synthesize and test for a given pathogen. This choice 23 

is limited by synthesis capacity, assay throughput and the cost of follow-up characterization. 24 

Because AMP activity and liability profiles are target and assay dependent, a library suited 25 

to Acinetobacter baumannii may be poorly matched to Candida albicans , Pseudomonas 26 

aeruginosa or Staphylococcus epidermidis [3] . A generator that treats these targets as 27 

interchangeable can expand sequence space without improving the pool chosen for the 28 

assay. 29 

AMPs make this selection problem especially difficult. These molecules are often short, 30 

charged and amphipathic; many disrupt microbial membranes [4] , and others act through 31 

intracellular or immunomodulatory mechanisms [1] . Small changes in residue composition 32 

or patterning can shift potency, solubility, selectivity, haemolysis, mammalian cytotoxicity 33 

and serum stability [3] . This sensitivity makes AMPs attractive design substrates and 34 

complicates pathogen-specific selection. A generator can produce plausible AMP-like 35 

sequences and still fail to bias the candidate pool toward the pathogen being tested. 36 

Most generative AMP workflows generate first and address pathogen specificity later. 37 

Deep generative models can produce potent AMP-like sequences [5] , and recent generative 38 

AI pipelines have further broadened de novo AMP discovery [6] . They produce sequences 39 

with broad antimicrobial features, then use predictors, filters or manual rules to select 40 

candidates for synthesis [3] . By then, the candidate library has already been formed. For 41 

pathogen-specific discovery, the target organism should shape the candidate pool as it is 42 

made. 43 

A pathogen target is more than an activity label. Broad labels such as antibacterial, 44 

antifungal, antiviral and antiparasitic distinguish major activity classes; they do not 45 

define a species or strain. Genome-derived features can encode organism-level sequence 46 

composition and strain-associated properties. Strain descriptions can capture taxonomy, 47 

source, phenotype and resistance-associated context. Together, these inputs bring target-48 

organism information into peptide generation. 49 

Here, we develop AMPHORA to test whether pathogen context can guide antimicrobial 50

Results 61 

A decodable short-peptide generator anchors the design space 62 

Before introducing pathogen context, we first established a generator whose outputs could 63 

be evaluated as short-peptide candidates. Short AMPs and smORF-derived peptides 64 

concentrate functional information in local motifs, charge placement, hydrophobic and 65 

polar patterning, amphipathicity and partial disorder. A peptide-focused representation 66 

was needed before target information could be tested as a generative signal. 67 

The peptide branch of AMPHORA combines a short-peptide encoder, deterministic 68 

biophysical priors, decodable latent compression and a sequence–structure latent-flow 69 

generator (Fig. 1). This separated baseline peptide generation from the pathogen-70 

conditioning tests. Peptide hidden states were mapped into a 256-dimensional latent 71 

sequence and decoded back into canonical amino-acid strings, producing valid outputs. 72 

Conditional effects could therefore be evaluated directly in generated peptides. 73 

We next compared four unconditional flow backbones under the same evaluation 74 

setting: sequence-only, global-structure latent, joint sequence–structure and pair-support 75 

dual-stream models (Fig. 2a). The pair-support dual-stream model emerged as the most 76 

balanced backbone, preserving local peptide grammar and predicted structural features. 77 

In the fixed-25-amino-acid benchmark, it achieved PRDC precision 0.934, PRDC recall 78 

0.403, uniqueness 1.0000 and an APEX-predicted MIC < 100 𝜇 M fraction of 0.070 (Fig. 79 

2b and Supplementary Table 5). This benchmark selected a backbone that could generate 80 

short, valid and AMP-like candidates before any pathogen information was introduced. 81

Pathogen context redirects generated peptide pools beyond target class 84 

We next tested whether pathogen context redirected generated peptide pools beyond target 85 

class. Target-class-only generation provided the baseline, as antibacterial, antifungal, 86 

antiviral and antiparasitic labels already organize AMP sequence space. These labels do not 87 

specify the species or strain used in an assay. We compared matched full conditioning with 88 

target-class-only, class-plus-genome, class-plus-text and shuffled genome/text conditions. 89 

Full conditioning increased macro F1 from 0.335 with target class alone to 0.391 (Fig. 90 

2c and Supplementary Table 6). Target class plus genome reached 0.373, and target class 91 

plus text reached 0.379. Genome and text each improved assignment beyond target class, 92 

and the full condition gave the strongest signal. This ordering placed pathogen-derived 93 

information above the broad class label and showed that both modalities contributed to the 94 

generated pool. 95 

Shuffling genome and text conditions weakened this signal. Global reassignment 96 

reduced macro F1 to 0.270, below the target-class-only baseline (Fig. 2e and Supplementary 97 

Table 11). The drop indicates that the matched signal was not explained by a generic 98 

AMP prior or target-class labels alone. Within-class shuffling stayed closer to matched 99 

generation, showing that part of the readout follows broad class structure in the paired 100 

corpus. Thus, pathogen context added information beyond class labels, while broad activity 101 

class remained a major organizer of AMP space. 102 

Class-wise recall showed detectable bacterial, fungal and viral signals; parasite support 103 

was too sparse for a class-level conclusion (Fig. 2d). Thus, aligned pathogen context 104 

altered the generated pool before any post-generation filtering or external scoring. 105 

Genome and text conditions shape different parts of generation 106 

Genome and text conditions changed generation in different ways. We isolated each channel 107 

by generating paired peptides with the same target class, length, initial noise, solver settings 108 

and seed, then swapping either the genome or text condition. Paired outputs differed only 109 

in the exchanged condition channel. This same-noise design allowed sequence changes to 110 

be attributed to the swapped pathogen input. 111

Pathogen responsiveness is target dependent 126 

Antimicrobial screens ultimately ask whether candidates work against a species or strain. 127 

We next tested whether matched conditioning enriched generated pools at the species level. 128 

This readout is more demanding than class assignment because species reference sets 129 

are smaller and less balanced. It also tests the central claim more directly: a pathogen-130 

conditioned generator should change candidate pools for individual targets, not only for 131 

broad AMP classes. 132 

Each species was represented by multiple peptide prototypes, reducing dependence 133 

on a single reference centroid. Fresh matched candidates were generated for 12 species, 134 

with three seeds and 1,000 peptides per species and seed. Across the panel, mean diagonal 135 

enrichment was 0.00613 and the mean specificity ratio was 1.075. The pooled effect 136 

was small, but the target-by-target pattern was informative. Candida albicans (0.0187), 137 

Aspergillus fumigatus (0.0146) and Staphylococcus epidermidis (0.0112) showed the 138 

strongest matched enrichment. Klebsiella pneumoniae fell below baseline (-0.0067) (Fig. 139 

3a). 140 

This pattern showed that species-level enrichment was target dependent across the 141 

12-species panel. 142

Conditioned libraries retain novelty and peptide-like support 153 

We next evaluated whether target-shaped libraries retained sequence novelty and peptide-154 

like support. The analysis focused on validity, uniqueness, training-set similarity, AMP-155 

neighbourhood support and predicted structural features. 156 

Matched full-condition generation produced valid, unique peptide sequences. Exact-157 

match novelty against the training set was 0.9997, and 96.4% of generated peptides 158 

remained below 70% nearest-training identity (Fig. 3d). These two measures separate 159 

exact memorization from near-neighbour similarity. Generated peptides also retained 160 

local AMP-reference support without concentrating in a narrow training-like cluster (Fig. 161 

3d). The conditioned libraries therefore remained near AMP-like regions while retaining 162 

substantial novelty relative to the training set. 163 

We next tested whether this sequence-level novelty was accompanied by predicted 164 

structural support. OmegaFold-derived models had a mean pLDDT of 67.95, compared 165 

with 73.72 for curated references, and contact density remained close to the reference 166 

panel (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 10). A lineage-level comparison showed higher 167 

predicted structural confidence, lower disorder and higher contact density in the final 168 

AMPHORA with orthogonal condition slots (AMPHORA-OS) checkpoint than in the no-169 

condition Stage 3 predecessor (Supplementary Table 9). Pathogen conditioning preserved 170 

the sequence–structure regime selected during backbone development. 171 

Pathogen-context shifts therefore occurred within a novel, peptide-like design space. 172 

They were not driven by copying, invalid decoding or motif collapse. The resulting libraries 173 

provided diverse, novel and peptide-like candidate pools for computational evaluation and 174 

experimental follow-up. 175

Discussion 176 

AMPHORA moves pathogen specificity from post-generation filtering into the generative 177 

step of antimicrobial peptide design. The central bottleneck is not simply whether a 178 

model can produce AMP-like sequences, but how the candidate-library distribution should 179 

be shaped for a defined pathogen under a finite experimental budget. By conditioning 180 

generation on target class, genome-derived features and strain-description text, AMPHORA 181 

makes pathogen context part of library construction. This reframes AMP generation as 182 

a pre-synthesis allocation problem in which the target organism shapes the peptide pool 183 

sampled before screening. 184 

The evidence supports a controlled computational claim. A decodable short-peptide 185 

generator first established a peptide-like design space in which conditional effects could be 186 

measured. Matched, partial and shuffled controls then showed that aligned pathogen inputs 187 

changed generated peptide pools beyond broad activity labels. Same-noise counterfactuals 188 

and condition-slot analyses separated the two conditioning routes: strain-description text 189 

mainly altered amino-acid choices, whereas genome-derived features acted more strongly 190 

through predicted structural properties. Species-level analyses extended the readout beyond 191 

class labels and showed target-dependent enrichment across pathogens. Novelty, identity, 192 

AMP-neighbourhood and predicted-structure checks showed that these shifts occurred 193 

within a diverse, non-memorized and peptide-like candidate space. 194 

This contribution differs from scaffold optimization and reservoir mining. Scaffold 195 

optimization begins with a known peptide and searches locally for improved variants. 196 

Reservoir mining searches existing biological sequence space for candidates that are already 197 

present. AMPHORA instead asks how a target organism should shape de novo candidate-198 

library construction before synthesis. This distinction matters because antimicrobial 199 

discovery is often limited by assay capacity. A computational generator is useful only if it 200 

changes the composition of the small library that will actually be tested. 201 

The uneven species-level response defines the operating range of the current model. 202 

Matched context produced clearer enrichment for some pathogens than for others, and 203 

within-class shuffling showed that broad activity labels still explain part of the generated 204 

signal. These results place AMPHORA at the level of candidate-pool allocation: it uses 205 

pathogen-associated genome and text information to shape which peptides enter a finite 206 

design set before synthesis. Predicted structural analyses further show that this reshaping 207

Figure 1. AMPHORA architecture for pathogen-context-conditioned antimicrobial peptide generation. a, Overview of the AMPHORA framework. A short-peptide or smORF sequence is encoded by smORFLLM, a peptide-focused encoder augmented with deterministic biophysical priors, to produce peptide latent representations that remain decodable through a learned latent compressor and sequence decoder. In parallel, pathogen context is represented through three aligned inputs: target-class embeddings, genome-derived features and strain-description text embeddings. These pathogen-context encoders are aligned with peptide representations and injected as orthogonal condition slots into a sequence–structure latent flow-matching generator. The generator uses a dual-stream pair-support backbone, with a sequence stream derived from smORFLLM embeddings and a structure stream derived from OmegaFold embeddings, to produce pathogen-context-shaped antimicrobial peptide libraries for downstream computational evaluation and experimental screening. 

b, Training and generation stages. The smORFLLM pretraining backbone learns short-peptide representations from smORF and small-protein sequences capped at 128 amino acids, with biophysical priors including charge, disorder and length-related descriptors. In Stage 1, peptide sequences, pathogen genomes and strain-description texts are encoded by smORFLLM, LucaOne and BioLinkBERT, respectively, and projected into a shared peptide-conditioned embedding space using contrastive alignment. In Stage 2, smORFLLM residue-level hidden states are compressed into a 256-dimensional decodable latent sequence; a sequence decoder preserves amino-acid reconstructability, whereas a preservation head retains information from the original smORFLLM hidden states. In Stage 3, an unconditional sequence–structure latent flow is trained over sequence and OmegaFold-derived structure latents, with residue-pair support used to select the dual-stream pair-support backbone. In Stage 4, target pathogen class, genome features and description text are assembled by a condition builder, 𝑐 = 𝐵 𝜂 ( 𝑦, 𝑔, 𝑢 ), and used to guide conditional latent-flow generation from noise to peptide latent states, yielding pathogen-context-shaped AMP candidates.

Study design and training stages 219 

AMPHORA was trained through four stages with separate objectives. Stage 1 aligned 220 

peptide, genome and strain-description text representations on a curated conditional 221 

antimicrobial peptide (AMP) corpus. Stage 2 compressed peptide hidden states into a 222 

decodable 256-dimensional latent sequence. Stage 3 trained unconditional latent-flow 223 

generators and selected the sequence–structure backbone used for conditional modelling. 224 

Stage 4 introduced target-class, genome and text conditions through orthogonal condition 225 

slots and evaluated matched, partial and shuffled condition settings. Unless otherwise 226 

stated, peptide sequences used the 20 standard amino acids and were capped at 128 residues. 227 

Detailed objectives, optimization settings and checkpoint-selection rules are provided in 228 

Supplementary Methods. 229 

Peptide and pathogen-condition corpora 230 

The peptide representation model was pretrained on a short-peptide/smORF corpus rather 231 

than on AMP activity labels. Preprocessing started from a representative FASTA containing 232 

390,709,416 sequences assembled from public short-protein/smORF resources and internal 233 

smORF collections. MMseqs2 cluster-consistent splitting at 50% sequence identity 234 

produced 386,797,786 training rows, 1,951,071 validation rows and 1,960,384 test rows [9] .235 

Length-aware sampling retained all sequences of length at most 50, sequences of length 236 

51–90 with probability 0.5 and longer sequences with probability 0.2. 237 

The conditional corpus was derived from the AMP-positive subset of ESCAPE, a 238 

standardized multilabel AMP benchmark integrating more than 80,000 peptides from 27 239 

validated repositories [10] . The ESCAPE-derived AMP-positive source table contained 240 

21,409 unique AMP sequences. Peptide–target pathogen conditions were constructed by 241 

combining dbAMP and DRAMP target annotations with ESCAPE category labels, followed 242 

by manual pathogen/category checks. Exact dbAMP or DRAMP matches were treated 243 

as target-annotation evidence. Records supported only by ESCAPE category fallback 244 

labels were retained as target-class or category-level conditions rather than validated 245 

species-specific activity evidence. 246 

The formal conditional split contained 93,834 training rows and 4,910 validation 247 

rows, corresponding to 20,313 and 1,070 unique peptides. Exact train–validation peptide-248

Genome and text condition processing 253 

Manually checked conditions were expanded into peptide–pathogen–genome–text records. 254 

Each retained row was assigned a canonical species or taxon identifier, a representative 255

Conditional generation 327 

Stage 4 introduced target context while retaining the selected sequence–structure generative 328 

prior. The input dataset was the ESCAPE-derived conditional corpus. Final conditional 329

Target context was represented by target class, genome condition and text condition. 334

Species-level and APEX analyses 391 

The 12-species species-aware enrichment benchmark evaluated fresh matched generation 392 

from the final AMPHORA-OS checkpoint. Twelve target species were evaluated with three 393 

seeds and 1,000 generated peptides per species/seed. The canonical name Nakaseomyces 394 

glabratus was used for the species formerly referred to as Candida glabrata . Reference 395 

peptides were drawn from the combined train and validation conditional manifests for the 396 

same species after sequence de-duplication within species. Each species contributed 1,000 397 

reference entries; species with fewer than 1,000 unique reference sequences were sampled 398 

with replacement. 399 

Reference peptides were embedded with the Stage 4 clean latent readout. The 400 

preferred multi-prototype readout clustered each species into up to 10 prototypes with 401 

MiniBatchKMeans before assigning soft probabilities to generated peptides. Diagonal 402 

enrichment was the matched assignment probability to the target species minus the mean 403 

off-target assignment probability. Specificity ratio was the matched assignment probability 404 

divided by the mean off-target probability. The multi-prototype readout was used as 405 

the conservative main-text readout because it reduces sensitivity to a single centroid for 406 

heterogeneous AMP reference sets. 407
