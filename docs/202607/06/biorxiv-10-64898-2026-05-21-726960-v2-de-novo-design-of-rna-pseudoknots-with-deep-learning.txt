Abstract: RNA design has been hindered by the limited accuracy of 3D structure prediction. 30 

Here, we show that intricate RNA structures can be generated with current deep learning tools 31 

through accurate de novo design of pseudoknot secondary structures. In an Eterna competition 32 

involving 57 pseudoknots, generative AI methods matched experienced human designers in 33 

solving most blind challenges, evaluated by single-nucleotide-resolution chemical mapping, 34 

compensatory mutagenesis, and cryogenic electron microscopy. AI-generated molecules with 35 

accurate secondary structures formed well-ordered 3D folds stabilized by noncanonical tertiary 36 

interactions not modeled during design. Success was guided by an RNet foundation model 37 

trained on prior chemical mapping data, suggesting that some difficult RNA design tasks may be 38 

tractable without first solving RNA 3D structure prediction. 39

Main Text: Complex RNA structures underlie fundamental biological processes including 1

translation and viral replication as well as emerging biotechnologies and medicines. Building on 2

pioneering efforts in design by human experts ( 1), recent years have seen steady improvements 3

in the ability to design RNA structures ranging from mRNA vaccines ( 2) to ribozymes ( 3, 4) to 4

elaborate RNA origami ( 5), but these efforts have remained limited to design of simple RNA 5

secondary structures, re-design of previously known RNA structures, or the assembly of a 6

limited repertoire of well-characterized tertiary motifs. Deep learning-based design has been 7

successful in designing RNA molecules with structures related to previously characterized RNA 8

but with highly distinct sequences ( 6–8). However, de novo design of novel structures, while 9

routine for proteins ( 9, 10 ), has not been achieved for RNA. Progress in RNA design has been 10 

limited by the poor accuracy of RNA secondary and tertiary structure prediction methods, 11 

particularly for synthetic molecules ( 11 –13 ), for which sequences and structures of 12 

evolutionarily related molecules are unavailable. 13 

Pseudoknots are complex secondary structures in which nucleotides in the loops of stems form 14 

base pairs with loops outside their stems ( 14 ). An increasing number of natural pseudoknots have 15 

been shown to function as ribozymes, riboswitches, and ribosomal frameshifting signals through 16 

the formation of complex 3D topologies with well defined stems ( 15 , 16 ). However, novel 17 

pseudoknots have only been automatically designed for a special class of RNA origami 18 

molecules ( 5, 17 ) and have not been achieved with general tertiary motif assembly ( 18 ). In 2024, 19 

participants of the ‘OpenKnot’ challenges on Eterna ( 19 ) began to achieve accurate design of 20 

novel pseudoknots, as assessed and refined through experiments on millions of RNA molecules 21 

whose secondary structures were evaluated by their nucleotide-by-nucleotide reactivity to 22 

selective 2´ hydroxyl acylation read out with primer extension (SHAPE) ( 20 –22 ). At the same 23 

time, de novo RNA design algorithms based on deep learning were proposed but remained 24 

experimentally untested in their application to novel secondary and tertiary structures ( 23 , 24 ). 25 

These developments motivated the extension of the OpenKnot challenge to explicitly compare 26 

the design performance of these new AI algorithms to experienced Eterna participants ( Tables 27 

S1-S2 ). 28 

Benchmarking AI design against experienced humans 29 

Round 1 of the extended OpenKnot challenge invited six AI methods to submit designs for 30 

SHAPE experiments, in which single-stranded and conformationally flexible nucleotides are 31 

preferentially acylated at their 2´-hydroxyl groups and base paired nucleotides are typically 32 

protected and exhibit low reactivity ( Fig. 1A-E ). The 17 Round 1 secondary structures were 33 

drawn from 11 RNAs whose experimental 3D structures had been deposited in the Protein 34 

Databank (PDB) as well as six synthetic pseudoknots proposed by Eterna participants based on 35 

earlier partially successful attempts at design ( Tables S3-S4 and fig. S1A ). For several of the 11 36 

PDB-derived targets, the natural sequences were known to form alternative states. For example, 37 

in the Round 1 target W03, a long stem in an RNA sensing cyclic diguanosine monophosphate in 38 

C. acetobutylicum (cdiGMP-II riboswitch ( 25 )) appeared mostly unformed in the absence of the 39 

small molecule ligand, as assessed by SHAPE ( Fig. 1D ). Therefore, even for cases with natural 40 

sequences, the challenge was to discover sequences that might stabilize the target pseudoknot for 41 

the RNA in solution without stabilizing partners. Up to 20 submissions were accepted from each 42 

AI method as well as from each Eterna participant; some methods that required input 3D 43 

backbones ( Table S2 ) were not able to make designs for the 6 targets without PDB structures. 44 

Designs throughout the OpenKnot challenge were probed in the absence of small-molecule 45

We observed a wide range of performance, with many Eterna designs achieving experimental 4

SHAPE profiles with the patterns of high and low reactivity matching the target profile of 5

unpaired and paired nucleotides ( Fig. 1B and 1E ). The agreement of each design's experimental 6

SHAPE profile with its target secondary structure was quantified using the OpenKnot score, 7

which computes the percentage of nucleotides whose SHAPE reactivity matches expectation 8

(Materials and Methods ); scores range from 0 to 100. A cluster of Eterna designs surpassed a 9

cutoff of success of 90% ( Figs. 1F and 1G ; Fig. S3A ; Data S1 ); this cutoff was set based on the 10 

inherent experimental uncertainty in the measurements ( 19 ) and an error rate of ~10% of SHAPE 11 

methods in assigning secondary structure in RNA’s with known structures ( 26 , 27 ). Eterna 12 

participants responsible for these high-scoring designs used a combination of in-game tools, 13 

intuition, and diverse design strategies ( Supplementary Text ). Most AI methods (Rosetta, 14 

Rosetta-LoRes, 3DRNA, gRNAde, MPNN-RFdiff; Table S2 ) did not pass the score cutoff and 15 

underperformed Eterna participants in Round 1 ( Figs. 1E–H ; Supplementary Text ). One 16 

exception was MPNN-fixbb, a message-passing neural network that designs sequences for a 17 

fixed input backbone (analogous to ProteinMPNN ( 28 , 29 )), which improved on average over 18 

the W03 starting sequence ( Figs. 1C, 1G ). Many of the top MPNN-fixbb designs, as well as 19 

some top scoring Eterna designs, were quite different from the starting sequence (sequence 20 

identity < 50%; see Fig. 1F ). In addition, all designs with >90% sequence identity to the starting 21 

sequence scored under 80%, suggesting the importance of shifting away from the natural 22 

sequence. 23 

At the completion of Round 1, a neural network RNet, trained on chemical mapping of 1M 24 

previous RNAs ( 21 ), was observed to give SHAPE profiles that gave simulated scores largely 25 

reproducing experimental scores, especially for poorly performing designs ( Fig. S4) . This 26 

observation suggested that RNet modeling might allow for prospective filtering of designs. The 27 

17 Round 1 targets were then posed again to all designers in a Round 2. Eterna designers, who 28 

were given access to RNet in the interactive interface, and AI design methods that were updated 29 

to take into account RNet (gRNAde, MPNN-RFdiff, MPNN-fixbb, and the new codesign-RFdiff; 30 

Materials and Methods ) did notably better in this round, e.g., on the W03 cdiGMP-II 31 

riboswitch target ( Fig. 1I and Fig. S3B ). Over all 17 targets, all the tested AI methods and Eterna 32 

human participants were able to achieve scores above 90 on at least 80% of the targets that they 33 

designed (light colored bars, Fig. 1J ), and there was no statistically significant difference 34 

between methods. For both Rounds 1 and 2, an alternative Z-score-based assessment modeled on 35 

procedures used in the Critical Assessment of Structure Prediction ( 12 , 13 ) gave similar rankings 36 

(Figs. S5A-B ). 37 

Generalization to novel pseudoknot targets 38 

The results above indicated that AI designers and Eterna participants could learn from 39 

explorations in prior designs to improve from Round 1 to Round 2, on the same set of 17 targets. 40 

As a test of the generalization of the resulting methods, we carried out a Round 3 with 20 new 41 

pseudoknots as target secondary structures ( Figs. 2A-C ; Fig. S1C ; Table S5 ). Compared to 42 

Rounds 1 and 2, fewer targets (5 vs. 11) were drawn from RNAs with experimental 3D structures 43 

in the PDB, to mitigate any effects of training set memorization for the AI methods. An 44 

additional 5 target secondary structures were drawn from Pseudobase, an expert-curated database 45

Compensatory mutagenesis confirms designed base pairs 29 

The results of Rounds 3 and 4 suggested that both experienced human designers as well as AI 30 

methods had improved to the point that they could design natural and non-natural pseudoknots 31 

without progressive refinement. However, the evaluation of these designs was based on a 32 

predetermined but arbitrary cutoff of 90 for the OpenKnot score and the assumption that SHAPE 33 

measurements accurately monitor base pairing status. Caveats for SHAPE interpretation have 34 

been described ( 26 , 27 , 32 , 33 ), and it is possible that nucleotides whose protection from SHAPE 35 

appeared consistent with the target secondary structure were instead forming alternative pairings. 36 

To test this possibility, we brought to bear an independent approach based on comparative 37 

mutagenesis, called mutate-map-rescue read out by high throughput sequencing (M2R-seq) ( 33 ,38 

34 ), illustrated for a gRNAde design of P20 (‘Kissing Multiloops’) from Round 3 in Fig. 3A-F .39 

If two nucleotides form a base pair in a putative RNA structure, mutation of either nucleotide 40 

should disrupt the pair and produce SHAPE perturbations near the site of mutation ( Fig. 3A–C; 41 

Fig. S6 shows detailed data for representative designs); reactivity changes at the unmutated 42 

partner can also occur but are not always evident, since the unmutated nucleotide may remain 43 

stacked or form alternative interactions ( 33 , 35 , 36 ). However, if the two nucleotides are mutated 44 

at the same time so as to flip the original base pair (e.g., C-G to G-C), the RNA’s structure 45

Cryo-EM reveals novel 3D folds and noncanonical interactions 18 

To learn if these novel pseudoknots might correspond to novel three-dimensional structures, we 19 

subjected several designs to cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM). Out of the non-natural 20 

secondary structures in Round 3, we prioritized designs for the Eterna-proposed target P20 21 

(‘Kissing Multiloops’, Fig. 2A , Fig. 3 , and Fig. 4A ) due to the short lengths of single-stranded 22 

linkers, which we reasoned would restrict the RNA’s 3D conformations sufficiently to enable 23 

their structural characterization via cryo-EM. Modeling in AlphaFold 3, trRosettaRNA, and other 24 

RNA 3D structure prediction algorithms also suggested that the P20 designs would form well-25 

defined folds, though 3D modeling confidence was poor ( Fig. 4B ). To increase the visibility of 26 

the designs in cryo-EM micrographs and to break any pseudosymmetry that might preclude high 27 

resolution refinement, we embedded the top P20 designs inside a recently developed circularly 28 

permuted group II intron scaffold ( 37 ). 29 

Four designs (the starting sequence, Rosetta, Eterna, and Struct2SeQ) appeared aggregated or 30 

unfolded by size exclusion chromatography during purification and were therefore not subjected 31 

to microscopy. These were also the designs that exhibited M2R-seq stem-wise recoveries less 32 

than 80%, suggesting that base pairing stability was important for retaining folded samples. The 33 

remaining four were imaged successfully ( Fig. 4C-E , Figs. S8-S11 , Table S6 , and Movies S1 34 

and S2 ). The MPNN-RFdiff design, which gave an M2R-seq stem-wise recovery of 80%, formed 35 

a dimer under conditions used for cryo-EM ( Materials and Methods ); all seven target stems 36 

were present but P4 'kissing' interactions were formed between molecules rather than within each 37 

molecule ( Figs. S8-S11 , Movie S2 , Supplementary Text ). 38 

The other three designs, from Struct2SeQ-SHAPE, MPNN-fixbb, and gRNAde, had M2R-seq 39 

stem-wise recoveries of 100%. All three resolved as monomers in cryo-EM, with map 40

sharpening to 4.8 Å, 4.0 Å, and 2.9 Å, respectively. In all three maps, stems P1-P7 and 42 

interconnecting linkers were clearly visible before sharpening ( Figs . 4C -E). These maps enabled 43 

the modeling of all coordinates, initially by unbiased manual tracing that was blind to the 44

RNA design without 3D structure prediction 7

Reliable de novo design of complex RNA pseudoknots is now achievable through deep learning. 8

In the OpenKnot AI challenge, automatic AI methods became competitive with experienced 9

human designers from Eterna in less than one year, with both kinds of approaches solving over 10 

95% of 57 pseudoknot design targets, as tested by SHAPE mapping and compensatory rescue 11 

experiments involving approximately 50,000 sequences. Progress in the analogous problem of de 12 

novo protein design has been driven by the use of accurate computational methods for 3D 13 

protein structure prediction ( 9, 10 , 38 ), but such tools remain unavailable for RNA ( 11 –13 ). 14 

Instead of relying on 3D structure prediction tools, the MPNN, gRNAde, and Struct2Seq 15 

frameworks here leveraged a model RNet trained on prior Eterna chemical mapping data that 16 

were mainly sensitive to secondary structure. Aside from this shared use of RNet, these three AI 17 

frameworks used different variants of deep learning ( Table S2 ), and our results were not able to 18 

discriminate with statistical confidence which of these AI methods was better than others. 19 

Instead, these three frameworks may be best deployed in combination, as sequence-space 20 

analysis shows that AI methods sample tight, mutually disjoint regions, providing 21 

complementary coverage of design space ( Fig. S14 ). Further experimental tests of these and 22 

other emerging methods ( 7, 39 –41 ) will be needed to clarify which frameworks will be most 23 

appropriate for these and future RNA design tasks. 24 

The AI-generated molecules probed here by cryo-EM displayed intricate noncanonical tertiary 25 

interactions, which are critical for sophisticated functions in natural RNA molecules ( 42 –44 ) but 26 

were not predicted a priori . On one hand, predictive modeling and design of such high resolution 27 

details will be important for designing novel RNA catalysts and aptamers and therefore remains 28 

an important challenge. On the other hand, the appearance of noncanonical interactions without 29 

explicit design suggest that other problems, such as re-design of machines like the ribosome and 30 

RNA polymerase ribozymes ( 45 , 46 ) or discovery of nonredundant folds to augment RNA 31 

structure databases ( 47 ), might be immediately accelerated through computational design guided 32 

by accurate secondary structure prediction, without also requiring high accuracy in prospectively 33 

modeling 3D interactions in atomic detail. 34
